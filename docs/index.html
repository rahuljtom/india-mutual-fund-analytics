Case study · 17,345,719 daily fund prices from AMFI · India · 2018 to 2025

How should an Indian saver choose an equity mutual fund?

17 million raw price records, parsed from a semi-structured text report, loaded into a typed SQL warehouse and tested against what actually happened. The question: which choices change the outcome, and which are noise?

Recommendation

Three rules, in order of how much the evidence supports them. Descriptive analysis of historical data, not investment advice.

  1. Buy the Direct plan. The Regular plan of the same scheme returned a median 1.33 points a year less across 365 matched pairs. A monthly SIP of ₹10,000 for 10 years at 12% gross loses about ₹1.5 lakh (6.8%); over 20 years ₹13.6 lakh (14.8%).
  2. Match the fund to the time you can leave it. Held for one year, equity funds lost money in 19.5% of windows; for three years 0.4%, for five years under 0.1%. Money needed within three years does not belong here. Small-cap funds earned the most but fell the most (median deepest fall -48% against about -37% for most categories).
  3. Do not pay for a past-performance screen. In a walk-forward test, picking last two years' top quartile beat the category by only +0.29 points a year (better in 3 of 5 years); the funds on the avoid list did +0.74 points better, and the low-volatility third -1.80 points.

1 · The source was a text report, not a table

AMFI publishes prices as a semi-structured text file with category and fund-house headings between the data lines. Turning it into something safe to analyse took a parser that tracks state line by line, a typed warehouse with constraints, and a decision on every kind of bad value. 2 · The one edge that shows up everywhere: cost

Direct and Regular plans hold the same portfolio; the Regular NAV is lower because of the commission built into its costs. Gap measured on 365 matched pairs: median 1.33 points a year (range 0.07 to 2.35). The rupee figures below use that gap with the gross return as a scenario, not a forecast.

Value lost by holding the Regular plan: ₹10,000 a month, in lakh rupees

Costs compound: the loss share roughly doubles from 6.8% at 10 years to 14.8% at 20 years.

4 · Does a screen for "good" funds work?

Walk-forward test: on 31 December each year from 2020 to 2024, use only the trailing 24 months to pick funds within each category, then measure the next 12 months against the average fund in that category. Funds that vanished before the end are counted separately, and the excess is measured on survivors, which can only flatter the rules.

Next-year return minus the category average, by selection rule and year (percentage points)

Every rule changes sign across the 5 test years. The weakest, the lowest-volatility third, is below zero in 4 of 5 years. No rule is reliably above zero.

Of top-quartile funds, 31.5% were top-quartile again (25% by chance; one-sided p = 0.13); of bottom-quartile funds, 38.7% stayed at the bottom (one-sided p = 0.01). Weak funds persist a little; strong funds do not. 8.8% of bottom-quartile funds had no price by the end of 2025 against 1.4% of top-quartile ones, which fund tables built from today's funds would hide.

5 · What would change this recommendation

  • One market regime. Eight years with one crash and a strong recovery. A multi-year bear market would lengthen the holding period needed to avoid a loss.
  • Costs are inferred from NAVs. The Direct–Regular gap is mostly the cost difference, not only; taxes, exit loads and fund size are not studied.
  • The backtest has five test years and overlapping funds. It shows no reliable edge in these data; it cannot prove none exists.
  • Categories change. A scheme that changed category keeps its latest one for its whole history.
  • Source terms. AMFI's terms of use for this data have not been reviewed here; check them before reuse.
